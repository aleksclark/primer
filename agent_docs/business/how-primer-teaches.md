# How Primer teaches

## The concise answer

Primer turns an adult's educational judgment into a repeatable daily loop with low educator burden. It selects work from a standards map and the student's prior evidence, asks the student to reason rather than merely answer, returns imprecise work for repair, adapts the next step to the misconception, reuses mastered skills in new settings, and records the artifact behind each mastery decision. The AI supplies patient attention and routine enforcement; the adult retains authority over content guardrails, worldview, the balance among instruction, projects, and investigation, and decisions to abandon a project or avenue of mastery.

## The learning loop

### 1. The adult sets the educational contract

The parent or teacher chooses curriculum, books, projects, schedule, and non-negotiable expectations. Primer converts those choices into a plan linked to standards and prerequisites. This distinguishes Primer from a child-directed chatbot: the student cannot silently lower the bar or wander away from the curriculum.

### 2. The planner chooses the next useful task

The session overseer considers:

- standards not yet introduced;
- prerequisites and current scope and sequence;
- weak or decaying mastery evidence;
- active project phases;
- formal-assessment gaps;
- explicit adult overrides.

The output is not merely “the next lesson.” It is the next opportunity to introduce, practice, transfer, or verify a skill.

### 3. A specialist tutor teaches within the subject

Each tutor carries a narrower instructional responsibility and can be configured with:

- standards and mastery criteria;
- common misconceptions;
- worked examples and multiple solution paths;
- appropriate scaffolds;
- the student's recent evidence in that subject.

The tutor begins with a task or question, observes the student's reasoning, diagnoses the likely misconception, and chooses the smallest helpful intervention. The preferred sequence is prompt, hint, decomposition, analogous example, then explicit modeling when needed. Socratic questioning is a tool, not a dogma.

### 4. Habit checks enforce quality across every subject

Before accepting work, Primer checks cross-cutting expectations:

- reasoning is shown;
- quantities include units and reasonable precision;
- claims cite a source, measurement, text, or observation;
- written work meets the expected grammar and mechanics;
- domain vocabulary is used precisely.

A failed check creates a correction loop. The student revises the work rather than receiving credit for partial compliance. Over time, these repeated demands form habits: an unsupported claim starts to feel unfinished, and a number without units starts to look wrong.

### 5. Feedback targets the error, not merely the answer

Primer should identify the first consequential error and ask the student to repair it. Feedback is:

- immediate enough that the reasoning is still active;
- specific to the student's work;
- calibrated so it does not remove the productive effort;
- followed by another attempt;
- explicit when questioning alone is no longer productive.

A correct final answer is insufficient when the reasoning is wrong. Conversely, a sound method with an arithmetic slip should not be treated as total non-understanding.

### 6. Skills are transferred into new contexts

Mastery is not established by repeating one item format. Primer deliberately looks for the same skill in another subject or practical setting:

- ratios reappear in recipes, budgets, and scale drawings;
- geometry reappears in CAD and construction;
- evidence reappears in history, science, and project journals;
- algebra reappears in spreadsheet formulas;
- experimental design reappears in gardens and material tests.

The student is asked to name and use the connection. This tests whether the skill can transfer rather than merely whether the original exercise pattern was memorized.

### 7. Real work supplies consequences and artifacts

Projects move a large portion of learning away from the screen. The student researches, designs, measures, builds, observes, documents, and presents. A physical result supplies feedback that a worksheet cannot: a structure can be out of square, a budget can be exceeded, and a prediction can fail.

Primer's role is to plan the phases, make academic demands visible inside the work, check calculations and explanations, and collect evidence. The student does the work; the system structures and assesses it.

### 8. Every mastery claim carries evidence

Primer records mastery per standard, not only a course percentage. Evidence may include:

- a corrected explanation from tutoring;
- a formal assessment item;
- an essay or journal entry;
- a drawing, model, photograph, or measurement;
- an oral defense reviewed by an adult;
- successful use of the skill in a later context.

A status changes only under a defined evidence rule. Confidence can fall when a skill is not used or when a later check exposes decay. The record should say what the student did, when, under what conditions, and who or what evaluated it.

### 9. The adult reviews decisions and exceptions

The adult sees session transcripts, flagged struggles, standards evidence, portfolio artifacts, and changes to the plan. Primer escalates when:

- the student remains stuck after several scaffold levels;
- work suggests a persistent misconception;
- a safety or welfare issue appears;
- the student's behavior prevents useful instruction;
- an assessment has high stakes;
- the model has low confidence in its evaluation.

The adult can override a plan or mastery decision. Abandoning a project or avenue of mastery requires adult approval. This is not an escape hatch; it is the governance model.

## What makes the mechanism defensible

The defensible product is not the language model. Models and generic study modes will commoditize. Primer's durable system is the combination of:

1. an adult-controlled curriculum and policy layer;
2. a standards and prerequisite graph;
3. longitudinal evidence per learner;
4. subject-specific tutoring behavior;
5. cross-subject quality checks;
6. transfer opportunities across activities and tools;
7. an off-screen project workflow;
8. auditable decisions and adult override.

Each interaction improves the evidence graph and determines the next task. The value accumulates across months, not in a single clever conversation.

## What must be validated

The mechanism is a product hypothesis until measured. Current real-world use is limited to newly introduced command-teacher and PrimerTV workflows; subject tutors, project orchestration, and mastery assessment remain planned. Pilot work should test:

- whether students actually complete correction loops rather than disengage;
- whether adults accept and understand mastery recommendations;
- whether skills transfer to unfamiliar tasks;
- whether standards decisions agree with independent human scoring;
- whether the system reduces parent planning and assessment time;
- whether learning gains exceed a credible comparison condition;
- whether benefits persist after model, content, and student variation.
