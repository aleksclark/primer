# Evidence for the instructional thesis

## The honest claim

Research strongly supports intensive tutoring, timely feedback, and mastery-oriented instructional practices. It does not yet establish that Primer, or LLM tutoring generally, produces the same effects as trained human tutors. The pitch should use prior research to justify the hypothesis and Primer pilots to prove the product.

## Tutoring evidence

### Best headline

Nickow, Oreopoulos, and Quan's peer-reviewed 2024 meta-analysis synthesized 89 randomized controlled trials of preK–12 tutoring and reported a pooled effect of **0.288 standard deviations**. Literacy and mathematics effects were similar at about 0.29 and 0.27 SD.

This is a large effect by education-research benchmarks, but it is an average across diverse programs, subjects, ages, tutors, doses, and outcome measures.

### What drives stronger results

Across modern reviews, tutoring tends to work better when it has:

- frequent sessions, commonly three or more per week;
- a consistent tutor;
- alignment with the student's curriculum;
- a small tutor-to-student ratio, usually no more than three learners;
- delivery during the normal learning day;
- sufficient duration, often ten weeks or more;
- trained tutors and structured materials;
- regular use of student data to adjust instruction.

These are product requirements, not merely citations. Primer must create consistent dosage, curriculum alignment, longitudinal context, and implementation visibility if it wants to inherit any of this evidence base.

### Modern examples

- Two Chicago Saga Education randomized trials with roughly 5,300 high-school students found mathematics effects ranging from **0.18 to 0.40 SD** and substantial reductions in course failure. The model used daily, intensive, usually 2:1 human tutoring.
- A technology-supported Saga model serving more than 4,000 students reported about **0.19 SD intent-to-treat** and **0.23 SD treatment-on-treated**, with lower staffing costs. It remained a blended tutoring program rather than an autonomous AI tutor.
- An online volunteer-tutoring randomized study in Italy reported about **0.26 SD** on a standardized test for disadvantaged middle-school students.
- Large district and national scale-ups have also produced much smaller or null effects. Nashville's district-operated program found modest reading gains and no mathematics effects; England's National Tutoring Programme generally produced about one month of additional progress or less.

### Scale caveat

Kraft, Schueler, and Falken's expanded tutoring meta-analysis reports that estimates for policy-relevant, large-scale U.S. programs are only about one-third to one-half as large as full-sample meta-analytic estimates. Implementation quality, attendance, staffing, and outcome choice matter.

The safe conclusion is not “tutoring always works.” It is:

> Well-designed, high-dosage tutoring is among the most consistently effective academic interventions, but effects shrink when dosage and implementation fidelity weaken.

## The Bloom two-sigma question

Bloom's 1984 paper made one-to-one tutoring famous by illustrating a two-standard-deviation difference over conventional instruction. This should not be used as Primer's promised outcome.

The underlying evidence came from two short dissertation studies using narrow researcher-made tests. The tutoring condition bundled tutoring with mastery quizzing, corrective feedback, retesting, specially trained tutors, and additional instructional time. Later reviews have not replicated a two-sigma average. Von Hippel's 2024 reconstruction explains that much of the original gap came from the mastery/feedback component and outcome design, not tutoring alone.

Use Bloom as historical motivation only, followed immediately by the modern evidence:

> Bloom posed the question of how to approximate individual tutoring at scale. Modern randomized evidence supports an average tutoring effect around 0.29 SD, not two sigma.

## Mastery-learning evidence

Kulik, Kulik, and Bangert-Drowns' 1990 meta-analysis of 108 controlled evaluations reported a mean effect around **0.52 SD** for mastery-learning programs. Slavin's stricter best-evidence review found a median around **0.25 SD** and essentially no effect on broad standardized measures. The disagreement is important:

- effects are stronger on assessments closely aligned with the taught material;
- mastery conditions often receive more time, feedback, and retesting;
- much of the literature is old and predates current research standards;
- broad transfer and standardized-test gains are less certain.

Primer should describe its approach as mastery-oriented and validate both aligned mastery and transfer to independent measures.

## Adaptive instruction evidence

Mindspark's computer-adaptive instruction produced about **0.37 SD in mathematics and 0.23 SD in Hindi** over 4.5 months in an Indian lottery-based randomized study. A later government-school scale-up reported approximately **0.22 SD mathematics and 0.20 SD Hindi** after 18 months at lower cost, with effects tied to usage and reduced when adult supervision weakened.

This supports the plausibility of adaptive, level-appropriate instruction at scale. It does not validate a specific LLM architecture or U.S. homeschool implementation.

## Claims the pitch can make

- Across 89 randomized preK–12 trials, tutoring produced an average effect of about 0.29 SD.
- Tutoring effects are strongest with frequent, sustained, curriculum-aligned sessions and small groups.
- Mastery learning and corrective feedback have a positive evidence base, though broad standardized-test effects are smaller and disputed.
- Adaptive instruction can produce meaningful gains, but implementation and usage determine results.
- Primer is designed to reproduce key mechanisms: immediate feedback, individualized pacing, repeated correction, longitudinal context, and frequent practice.
- Primer's own efficacy remains to be established through external assessments and well-designed pilots.

## Claims the pitch should not make

- Primer gives every student a two-sigma improvement.
- AI tutoring is equivalent to trained human tutoring.
- One-to-one instruction always beats small-group instruction.
- Mastery learning raises standardized scores by one standard deviation.
- Every logged interaction is valid evidence of mastery.
- Product engagement is the same as learning.

## Primer evidence plan

The first falsifiable outcome is progression on independently scored, state-aligned standards. For supplementary remediation, improvement should also become visible in school performance, although grades are an imperfect and teacher-dependent secondary measure. Current use is too early to support efficacy claims.

### Stage 1: measurement validity

Before claiming learning impact, establish that Primer measures accurately.

- Create externally scored tasks for the launch standards.
- Blind human reviewers to Primer's mastery decisions.
- Measure agreement, false mastery, and missed mastery.
- Test scoring consistency across model versions, demographics, writing styles, and accommodations.
- Use spot checks and anomaly detection to identify implausible jumps and novel cheating patterns.
- Keep the long-term objective of autonomous mastery evaluation while requiring stronger review for consequential or low-confidence decisions until reliability is known.

### Stage 2: feasibility pilot

Use a small cohort to establish:

- enrollment and completion;
- dosage achieved;
- correction-loop completion;
- parent burden and time saved against the founder's reported baseline of more than ten hours per week using a standard homeschool package;
- safety and escalation rates;
- pre/post change on an independent state-aligned assessment;
- student and parent retention.

Pre-register primary outcomes and success thresholds before analyzing results.

### Stage 3: comparative study

Compare Primer with a credible alternative, such as business-as-usual curriculum plus generic online practice. Match or randomize where feasible. Use an external standardized or validated assessment, not only Primer-generated questions.

Report:

- intent-to-treat effects;
- actual usage and treatment-on-treated analysis separately;
- attrition by group;
- subgroup results only when adequately powered;
- implementation costs per learner;
- adverse and null results;
- model/version and curriculum used.

### Stage 4: school evidence

For institutional sales, run independent studies in the intended setting. An effective homeschool implementation does not automatically establish classroom efficacy. Schools will need implementation evidence, accessibility and privacy readiness, and eventually evidence aligned with ESSA tiers.

## Investor interpretation

The research reduces pedagogical risk: Primer is built from mechanisms with substantial support. It does not remove execution or efficacy risk. The investment case should make the proof plan an asset: a system that records dosage, feedback loops, artifacts, and standards decisions can evaluate itself more rigorously than a generic chatbot if those records are validated against independent outcomes. Lack of progression on tested standards is the explicit failure condition.
