# Go-to-market strategy

## Principle

Primer should sequence markets by product fit and proof burden, not by headline market size. Start where one decision-maker feels the problem and can buy quickly. Homeschool families and parents seeking supplementary education can adopt the Primer LMS directly; co-ops and schools can adopt bounded programmes institutionally. Use those markets to improve the product and produce evidence while building integrations only when they unlock a defined customer.

## Phase 1: homeschool design partners

### Goal

Prove that the learning loop works in real households and that parents will return, pay, and trust the records.

### Customer

A narrow set of grade 5–8 homeschool families with a clear unmet need, not the whole sector. Recruit families that already use structured curriculum and can compare Primer with their current planning, practice, and tutoring stack.

### Motion

- founder-led interviews and onboarding;
- small paid design-partner cohort;
- high-touch observation of weekly use;
- direct collection of parent time saved and student work;
- explicit permission and privacy process for any research data;
- regular parent review sessions;
- no broad paid acquisition until activation and retention are understood.

### Product milestone

One coherent job must work end-to-end. At launch Primer must own curriculum planning, assessment evaluation, and standards/mastery records. The first proof can still be narrower: remediation against an ingested syllabus in one high-value subject can validate diagnosis, instruction, assessment, and longitudinal records before every subject is complete.

The full-system vision should not force the first SKU to be broad. A narrower product can prove the correction, transfer, and evidence loop sooner.

### Metrics

- household activation: first planned session and first accepted evidence artifact;
- weekly active learners and active adults;
- completed correction loops per session;
- planned versus completed learning sessions;
- 4-, 8-, and 12-week household retention;
- parent planning/assessment time before and after;
- independent agreement with mastery decisions;
- learning gain on an external measure;
- referral rate and reason for cancellation;
- contribution margin after model, media, and human support costs;
- time-to-value within the first one or two sessions;
- progress on independent state-aligned assessments.

## Phase 2: supplementary family use and trusted distribution

Expand the same family product to parents whose children attend public or private school. The first wedge is remediation: the parent ingests a syllabus through chat, Primer maps the student's gaps against it, and focused instruction aims to improve tested standards and school performance. Acceleration, projects, summer use, and homework support follow. This bypasses institutional procurement while creating evidence and parent advocates inside school communities. Parents own the records; initial success should be visible in external assessments and school grades without requiring record pushback.

Homeschool acquisition remains trust-heavy and fragmented. There is no complete national parent list. The scalable channels are aggregators and recommendations.

### Channels

- parent referrals and sibling/household expansion;
- homeschool co-op leaders;
- parent groups and family networks around public and private schools;
- microschool founders;
- curriculum reviewers and aligned creators;
- regional conventions where worldview fit is explicit;
- local and online homeschool communities;
- partnerships with complementary off-screen curricula;
- parent case studies showing real student work, not testimonials alone.

The product must make referral easy without exposing child data. Give parents a clear way to share the system, not their student's transcript.

### Package expansion

Use one longitudinal account and a clear responsibility ladder:

- **Base, $50/month:** two or three selected subjects, mastery evidence, and parent progress;
- **Core, $100/month:** full core academics, cross-subject planning, projects, and portfolio;
- **Premier, $300/month:** customized full-spectrum education, managed content, advanced/test-prep/executive-function support, and higher-touch reporting.

Base acquires and proves the learner relationship. Core expands the academic footprint. Premier consolidates tutoring, coaching, content, and planning spend for high-intent families and premium schools. Each upgrade must expose the added capability and usage rather than functioning as an arbitrary price fence.

### Messaging

Lead with the parent job:

- know what the student actually understands;
- stop carrying gaps forward;
- get patient feedback without surrendering curriculum control;
- turn projects and written work into standards evidence;
- spend less parent time planning and checking routine work.

“AI-powered” is implementation detail. “A disciplined tutor the parent directs” is the proposition.

## Phase 3: education-choice funding

ESA programs can make a premium product affordable, but each program has its own eligible expenses, vendor approval, payment administrator, and renewal process.

### State selection rubric

Prioritize states by:

1. funded homeschool participation, not theoretical eligibility;
2. grade and product eligibility;
3. direct-pay versus reimbursement rules;
4. marketplace administrator and integration burden;
5. award size and purchasing thresholds;
6. alignment cost for standards/reporting;
7. concentration of existing customers and partners;
8. regulatory stability.

Do not launch in every ESA state at once. Choose one or two where existing design partners can pull Primer through approval.

### Required operations

- per-state vendor-status register;
- eligible product descriptions and invoices;
- direct-pay or marketplace checkout support;
- family reporting suitable for the state's rules;
- clear separation of covered and non-covered services;
- monitoring of administrative-rule changes.

ESA funding should improve conversion and access. It should not mask weak household retention.

## Phase 4: co-ops and microschools

These organizations aggregate 10–30 learners around one decision-maker and often have the curricular flexibility of homeschool families.

### Offer

- self-serve organization setup with low seat minimums;
- working pricing hypothesis near $50 per learner per month plus included usage and metered overage, validated against inference costs;
- adult roles for founder, guide, tutor, and parent;
- group curriculum policy with individual pacing;
- simple rostering and CSV import;
- learner portfolios and standards reports;
- organization-level usage and progress view;
- family access without duplicating records;
- pricing in the existing curriculum budget rather than district-enterprise pricing.

### Why this is the bridge

It forces Primer to support multiple learners, staff roles, and institutional reporting without immediately requiring district procurement, state DPAs, or every SIS integration. It also creates reference sites for private schools.

## Phase 5: premium private and supplementary school pilots

### First school segment

Start with elite independent/boarding schools and receptive public-school programmes whose leaders value mastery, projects, family involvement, intervention, or individualized pacing. For elite schools, target parent-paid community access or a bounded learning-support/study-hall programme before tuition-embedded adoption. For public schools, sell a targeted $1,200 annual tutoring/intervention service, not a whole-school software license. Primer is itself an LMS, but the first institutional offer should integrate with the incumbent SIS, SSO, and LMS rather than force rip-and-replace.

Three motions are distinct:

- **parent-to-school:** even one learner can establish a useful supplementary pilot; multiple families and student preference create stronger bottom-up demand;
- **independent private school:** head-of-school or academic-leader sale, philosophy-first, smaller contract;
- **public school or network:** bounded programme with centralized academic, technology, privacy, and procurement review.

### Entry product

Sell a bounded use case:

- Base or Core remediation purchased by school families;
- $1,200 annual targeted school intervention;
- Premier learning support for elite-school families;
- boarding-school evening study hall and international/EAL support;
- project-based standards evidence;
- homeschool/remote programme;
- one-subject mastery pilot.

A bounded pilot is easier to evaluate and safer than asking a school to replace core instruction immediately.

### Pilot contract

Predefine:

- target learners and comparison group;
- implementation support;
- usage expectation;
- learning and operational outcomes;
- privacy and data-return terms;
- renewal price and decision date;
- product capabilities required for expansion.

## Phase 6: broader institutional adoption

Supplementary school pilots can begin earlier, while broad public-school adoption should follow only after Primer has:

- repeatable learner outcomes;
- independent evidence beyond testimonials;
- an institutional product and support model;
- district-ready privacy, security, accessibility, identity, and interoperability;
- sufficient gross margin to support long sales and implementation cycles.

The first public-school motion remains supplementary. Primer should launch through LTI while presenting a substantially easier secondary LMS experience for educators and students. Clever SSO is the first planned institutional connector because of its reach. Broader SIS, roster, and grade synchronization follow demonstrated adoption. Primer should pursue primary-LMS status only when widespread use has made it the de facto instructional system. Aleks Clark's direct Skyward, PowerSchool, Google Classroom, OAuth, Common Cartridge, standards, and grade/roster integration experience reduces technical execution risk, though district compliance, procurement, and efficacy requirements still apply.

## Flywheel

1. Household use produces longitudinal work and product insight.
2. Strong outcomes and parent referrals lower acquisition friction.
3. Co-ops and microschools aggregate households and create reference sites.
4. Institutional pilots produce implementation playbooks and stronger evidence.
5. Interoperability turns Primer from a replacement threat into an instructional layer.
6. Better evidence expands funding eligibility and buyer confidence.

This flywheel depends on consented, privacy-preserving analysis. Student records are not a marketing asset.

## Key risks

- a broad first product may take too long to become excellent in any subject;
- cheap generic AI tutors may compress willingness to pay;
- homeschool trust can be damaged by opaque AI decisions or weak content controls;
- worldview segmentation can fragment curriculum and support;
- ESA programs can change rules or administrators;
- a high-touch family product may have poor support economics;
- school expansion can distract from proving household retention;
- efficacy may shrink as implementation scales;
- per-learner inference costs may make an uncapped subscription uneconomic;
- fully autonomous mastery evaluation may miss novel cheating or scoring failures without strong anomaly detection and spot checks.
