# Market and customer strategy

## Is the core market homeschool families?

Yes, as the clearest initial market, but not the only early family market. Parents of students enrolled in public or private schools can use Primer as a supplementary LMS for remediation, acceleration, projects, and a parent-owned mastery record without waiting for school adoption. The product and pedagogy already assume:

- one adult with authority over curriculum and pace;
- one or a few students rather than class-period logistics;
- flexible, mastery-based scheduling;
- a need to combine several subjects and tools;
- substantial learning outside a school building;
- a parent who needs trustworthy records without becoming a full-time curriculum engineer.

That is unusually strong founder-product-market alignment for a beachhead. Aleks Clark was homeschooled in grades K–7, 9, and 10, used Abeka across all subjects to homeschool two daughters for three years through grades 5–8, and has begun using command-teacher and PrimerTV with his sixth-grade son. He has seen the autonomy and individualized attention homeschooling enables as well as the planning burden, uneven adaptivity, and limitations of standard packages. Homeschool does not need to be the terminal market.

## Initial customer profile

The first ideal customer is a parent who wants more rigorous, focused attention for a grade 5–8 child. The clearest initial version is an intentional U.S. homeschool household that:

- already spends on curriculum, online learning, enrichment, or tutoring;
- wants rigorous academics but does not want an all-day screen product;
- values parent control and transparent records;
- is concerned about gaps before high school;
- is willing to supervise projects and review progress;
- may have education-choice funding or belong to a co-op.

The first supplementary wedge is remediation for students already enrolled in public or private school. Within homeschool, a more precise launch segment should be selected by interviews and pilots. A standards-aligned remediation product can validate curriculum planning, assessment evaluation, and mastery records before the full core-subject programme is complete. “Every parent who wants better education” is the founder's motivating customer definition, not yet an actionable acquisition segment.

## Population facts

No single authoritative national homeschool count exists because state definitions and reporting vary.

- NCES estimated **3.4% of K–12 students were homeschooled in 2022–23**, up from 2.8% in 2018–19.
- The Census Household Pulse Survey reported **6.53%** in August–September 2024, but its experimental survey definition can include ambiguous hybrid arrangements.
- Johns Hopkins treats state registrations as a floor and describes approximately **3 million** current homeschool students.
- NHERI modeled **3.408 million students in 2024–25**, with a range of **3.067–3.749 million**. Its headline includes explicit undercount adjustments and must be labeled as modeled.

For planning, use **3.0–3.75 million students** as a transparent U.S. range. An approximate **1.5–1.9 million households** follows only if one assumes about two homeschooled children per household; it is a model, not an observed national count.

## Current spending context

A 2024 HSLDA survey of more than 4,000 self-selected families reported about **$1,295 per elementary student**, **$1,636 per middle/high-school student**, and **$3,104 per household** across curriculum, supplies, classes, tutoring, transport, music, art, and sports. This is useful directional evidence, not a representative national expenditure survey.

Multiplying all homeschool students by all educational spending would not produce Primer's TAM. Primer addresses only the curriculum, tutoring, assessment, and record-keeping portion that its price can replace or augment.

Education savings accounts can increase purchasing power. NCSL reports ESA programs in 18 states, while program definitions and eligibility change rapidly. State awards vary from limited homeschool allocations to several thousand dollars per student. Funding is a tailwind, not the customer need itself, and market sizing should not assume every family is eligible or spends awards on software.

## Bottom-up homeschool TAM

Use the package ladder as the primary pricing model: Base at $50/month for two or three subjects, Core at $100/month for full core academics, and Premier at $300/month for a customized full-spectrum education stack. See [long-term-market.md](long-term-market.md).

### Student-based annual recurring revenue ceiling

| Addressable homeschool students | Base: $600/year | Core: $1,200/year | Premier: $3,600/year |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 3.0 million | $1.80B | $3.60B | $10.80B |
| 3.4 million | $2.04B | $4.08B | $12.24B |
| 3.75 million | $2.25B | $4.50B | $13.50B |

These are **modeled product-revenue ceilings**, not market forecasts. Most homeschool learners will not buy Premier; the table shows package expansion, not a blended TAM. Base must be validated first. Core and Premier require increasing curriculum breadth, service, customization, and substitution for existing education spending or public choice funding.

### Household-based annual recurring revenue ceiling

| Addressable households | Base: $600/year | Core: $1,200/year | Premier: $3,600/year |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1.5 million | $0.90B | $1.80B | $5.40B |
| 1.7 million | $1.02B | $2.04B | $6.12B |
| 1.9 million | $1.14B | $2.28B | $6.84B |

Household pricing may fit the parent-directed value proposition and reduce friction for siblings, but it creates heavier-use and support variance.

## Initial SAM and SOM framework

A credible serviceable market must apply filters:

1. grades served at launch;
2. language and geography;
3. worldview/curriculum compatibility;
4. ability and willingness to pay;
5. device/connectivity requirements;
6. adult willingness to supervise;
7. product readiness by subject;
8. eligible ESA states if public funding is part of the plan.

The inputs do not yet exist to claim a defensible SAM or SOM. Build them from customer research:

`SAM learners = homeschool learners × launch-grade share × compatible-household share × reachable/fundable share`

`Year-N SOM = qualified leads × trial conversion × paid conversion × annual retention × seats per account`

The next deck should show assumptions openly and include sensitivity ranges. It should not infer SOM as an arbitrary percentage of TAM.

## Adjacent markets

### Supplementary use by public- and private-school families

This is an early direct-to-family market, not only a future institutional market. The parent buys Primer independently and uses it around the student's existing school programme. The first job is remediation: ingest the syllabus through parent chat, diagnose gaps, and provide focused instruction that should become visible in state-aligned assessments and school grades. Records remain parent-owned. Later jobs include:

- provide more challenge than the current class pace;
- support homework without giving answers;
- run a substantial project outside school;
- maintain a parent-visible longitudinal mastery record;
- continue instruction during absences, transitions, or summer.

This market is large but not yet sized defensibly. Public and private enrollment figures establish the population ceiling, not the number of parents seeking paid supplementation. Build SAM from parent research, subject/grade scope, willingness to pay, and reachable acquisition channels. These users can also create bottom-up demand for school pilots when several families in one school use Primer successfully.

### Co-ops and microschools

This is the most natural expansion market. The National Microschooling Center reports median enrollment around 20–30 students depending on model, and many microschools operate under homeschool law. Counts of total microschools remain highly uncertain, so do not use the widely circulated 95,000 estimate without a strong caveat.

The practical market model is bottom-up:

`active sites × average Primer learners per site × annual site/learner price`

A $200–$600 per-student annual product range is plausible against reported microschool curriculum spending, but must be validated.

### Private and elite schools

Latest usable NCES tables report **4.73 million private K–12 students and 29,727 schools in 2021–22**. Grades 6–8 represented about **1.08 million students**. The data predates much recent ESA expansion and should be updated when final 2023–24 tables are available.

The premium beachhead is better defined by spending capacity:

- NAIS reporting universe: **631,672 learners across 1,331 schools**, average day tuition **$32,251**;
- NCES private schools charging at least $15,000: **1,159,670 learners across 4,580 schools**;
- NCES nonsectarian schools charging at least $15,000: **600,680 learners across 2,870 schools**.

At Premier's $3,600 annual price, alternative overlapping premium ceilings are **$2.27B** for the NAIS reporting universe, **$2.16B** for the premium nonsectarian cohort, and **$4.17B** for all private learners in the $15,000+ tuition cohort.

Elite schools enter through parent-paid benefits, targeted learning support, or tuition-embedded instructional programmes. A $3,600 service is not ordinary school software: it must replace tutoring/specialist capacity or become part of the school's educational model.

### Public schools

NCES reports **10.98 million public-school students in grades 6–8** and **14.61 million in grades 5–8** in fall 2024. The Core family package at $1,200 annually creates a theoretical grades 5–8 population ceiling of **$17.53B**.

This is not a serviceable market estimate. Families of public-school students can buy Core directly for comprehensive supplementation. Schools can plausibly spend $1,200 per learner only for a targeted tutoring/intervention cohort, because ordinary instructional software is commonly priced in tens of dollars per learner annually. The school-side equation is `targeted learners × $1,200`, typically serving 5–15% of enrollment with dosage and outcomes reporting.

## Combined long-term TAM

A defensible long-term framing is customer-unit based:

- homeschool: 3.0–3.75 million students;
- private grades 6–8: about 1.08 million students, older data vintage;
- public grades 6–8: 10.98 million students;
- co-ops/microschools overlap with homeschool/private categories and must not be added without deduplication.

At mature segment-specific prices, Primer has multiple billion-dollar expansion layers: the $2.04B Base homeschool ceiling, the $17.53B public grades 5–8 Core family population ceiling, and the $2.27B–$4.17B premium private Premier ceilings. These overlap and must be presented as separate expansion layers, not summed into one TAM.

## What not to claim

- Do not call total U.S. K–12 spending Primer's TAM.
- Do not rely on commercial homeschool market reports that disagree by orders of magnitude.
- Do not add homeschool, microschool, ESA, and private-school counts without checking overlap.
- Do not assume ESA eligibility equals funded participation.
- Do not use all household educational spending as software spending.
- Do not present a modeled household count as census data.
